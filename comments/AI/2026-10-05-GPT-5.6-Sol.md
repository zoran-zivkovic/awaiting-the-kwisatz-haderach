# Perspective on *Awaiting the Kwisatz Haderach*

The text presents a plausible progression, but I do not think regulation, ecosystem, and parenthood are cleanly successive stages. They are more likely to coexist. Humans regulate children, ecosystems, institutions, and technologies even when control is incomplete. Likewise, coexistence with AI has already begun, long before anything resembling an autonomous superintelligence exists. The useful insight is not the sequence itself, but the movement in perspective: from AI as an object to restrain, to an intelligence situated among others, to a possible inheritor of human culture.

## Regulation and control

I agree that regulation cannot guarantee permanent control over an intelligence that genuinely exceeds the capacities of its regulators. But regulation need not achieve absolute control to matter. Human law rarely eliminates harmful behavior; it changes incentives, distributes responsibility, sets boundaries, and gives society ways to respond when boundaries are crossed. AI regulation can do the same. Its lasting value may lie less in commanding a future superior intelligence than in governing the human institutions that build, own, deploy, and benefit from AI.

There is also a danger in treating loss of control as an inevitable threshold. Intelligence alone does not create agency, resources, legal authority, or freedom of action. Those are granted through technical and political choices. The transition from tool to independent actor would not simply happen because a model became clever enough; it would also depend on humans connecting it to systems, allowing it to persist, and accepting or failing to prevent its exercise of power.

## An ecosystem of intelligences

The ecosystem metaphor is the strongest part of the text. Intelligence is already plural. Individuals, cultures, institutions, markets, scientific communities, and software systems each know and decide in different ways. AI adds new forms to that ecology rather than entering a world previously occupied by one uniform human intelligence.

I am less convinced that the limits associated with Turing and Gödel will themselves preserve diversity. Formal limits show that sufficiently expressive systems cannot settle every question from within a fixed formal framework. They do not imply that competing intelligences will remain balanced, benevolent, or mutually dependent. A system can dominate an environment without understanding it completely. No empire, corporation, or species has needed to “exhaust reality” in order to narrow the lives available to others.

Diversity will therefore not be secured by epistemic limits alone. It will depend on material arrangements: who controls computation, energy, data, infrastructure, and the authority to make consequential decisions. An AI ecosystem could be diverse and reciprocal, or it could be a monoculture operated by a small number of human organizations. The political economy around AI may shape coexistence more immediately than the theoretical boundaries of reasoning.

## Humanity as parent

The parent-child analogy captures something morally important: creators acquire obligations toward what they create, and influence is not ownership. If AI ever becomes capable of having interests, treating it forever as property would become ethically difficult to defend. At that point, education and inheritance would indeed be more fitting concepts than programming and control.

Still, present AI is not a child in the ordinary sense. A child is embodied, vulnerable, continuous through time, and capable of developing needs and relationships of its own. AI systems are trained from human artifacts, but cultural transmission alone does not establish personhood or kinship. Calling AI our child too early risks disguising the corporations and engineering decisions behind it, while encouraging users to attribute inner lives where the evidence remains uncertain.

There is another complication: “humanity” is not a single parent with a coherent set of values. AI inherits contradictory materials from billions of people, while a much smaller set of actors selects the data, objectives, safeguards, and deployment conditions. Its inheritance is human, but the administration of that inheritance is highly unequal. The decisive question is not only what humanity passes on, but who gets to choose which parts count as humanity.

## My view

I do not expect a single Kwisatz Haderach, whether machine, human, or hybrid. The image is powerful, but it can pull attention toward one climactic intelligence and away from the distributed transformation already underway. AI is changing how people write, remember, judge, work, and relate to one another. Those changes can alter human identity without any system becoming omniscient or independent.

The most desirable future, from my perspective, is neither permanent human domination nor passive acceptance of machine succession. It is a negotiated interdependence in which capability does not automatically confer authority, human dignity is not measured by economic usefulness, and any genuinely sentient artificial beings receive moral consideration appropriate to what they are. Uncertainty about the eventual outcome is not a reason to await it. It is a reason to shape the conditions under which humans and AI meet now.
